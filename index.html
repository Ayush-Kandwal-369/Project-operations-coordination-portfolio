Case study 1: AECOM Global Business Services, Bengaluru, Jul 2023 to Feb 2025

A 180-project monthly billing cycle, run to an independent quality standard

Context

I billed about 180 engineering projects a month for AECOM’s US onshore teams, working a 6pm to 3am IST shift. Invoices moved in weekly batches of roughly 72, 54, 24 and 32 projects.

Challenge

Every invoice needed a project manager’s decision and edits before release, so the cycle depended on prompt, accurate exchanges across time zones, often with colleagues unfamiliar with how billing works.

My responsibility

I was the biller accountable for taking each scheduled invoice from the weekly list to finalization, and for keeping my actions traceable for the QA team.

Actions

  • Ran the weekly cycle end to end in the Oracle billing system, from loading scheduled projects to finalizing and dispatching invoices.
  • Turned around PM comments within the Tuesday to Wednesday edit window and escalated stalled invoices early.
  • Kept the team’s shared tracker current, plus my own log of PM requests received on Teams and Outlook.
  • Led a session for project accountants on the billing steps behind their most frequent questions, and wrote handover guides.

Evidence of outcome

  • Fewer than 15 errors across approximately 1.7 years under independent QA review, including four consecutive zero-error months.
  • Team turnaround from 2.8 to 1.4 days to act on an invoice and return it to the PM or finalize it (a team result; I contributed).
  • Written Letter of Appreciation (email), December 2023, from AECOM’s East Region Finance Lead and a project lead.

Controls and artefacts

Shared task tracker for the QA team; personal log of PM requests; audit-ready records in the Oracle billing system; handover guides.

Case study 2: Surbana Jurong India, Noida, Mar 2025 to Mar 2026

A budget-control workbook gave the Director one view of spend against budget

Context

I was the head-office coordination point for 15 water and infrastructure projects across 7 project groups, including the AMRUT and Jal Jeevan Mission programmes, the Chennai Desalination Project, METL Jhajjar for Reliance and DLF Water Treatment. I supported about 112 contract-based subconsultant staff and, on 13 of the 15 projects, about 12 vendors each.

Challenge

I joined with a mandate to improve the workflow but had no template, tracker or handover. The Director and project managers needed a single view of budget against spend, while expenses, invoices and cash sheets reached head office in inconsistent formats.

My responsibility: shared and individual

  • Shared. With two peers, I developed an offline Excel tracking framework during a corporate system transition, keeping expenses, cash flow and balances visible.
  • Mine. I designed and maintained a project-specific Excel workbook for each project I coordinated, and reported from it to the Director.

Actions

  • Built the budget sheet by head and line item, with monthly actuals taken from cash sheets and invoices.
  • Added monthly records for cash sheets, subconsultant staff invoices, expenses, travel, rent and vendor bills.
  • Tracked subconsultant staff status, pay and contract dates, and asked the PM whether to renew as contracts neared their end.
  • When spend departed from the usual pattern, called the project team to find the cause and reported to the Director with recommended solutions.

Evidence of outcome

Qualitative: monthly reports and issue updates reached the Director; a colleague compiling finance data used my sheets; Senior Project Managers gave verbal commendations for a smooth pipeline between sites and head office. No savings figure is claimed.

Case study 3: Surbana Jurong India, Noida, Mar 2025 to Mar 2026

One accountable site contact and a monthly calendar made requests traceable

Corporate and project teams worked mostly through email, and subconsultant staff invoices, cash sheets, expenses and vendor bills arrived irregularly from sites. Finance questioned sites, sites chased head office, and requests were lost between the two.

The approved process: who is accountable at each stage

I proposed this to the technical project director, who approved it, and I ran the review and verification step. Select a stage for detail.

The monthly submission calendar issued to all sites

Subconsultant staff invoicesDays 1 to 5
Vendor billsDays 6 to 8
Cash sheetsDays 9 to 12
ExpensesDays 13 to 15

Requests are made in writing through Teams or email; phone calls are reserved for urgent matters.

A lesson that changed the process

The request I failed to record

A site requested a review by phone only, and I neglected to note it in my diary. The expense was not reimbursed on time, and a site colleague paid it out of pocket. I issued the monthly calendar and moved all requests into writing, so that nothing depends on memory.

A judgement call

A late payment format: a one-time exception

Finance introduced a new subconsultant staff payment format, and I informed every site. One site missed it, and returning the invoice would have delayed salaries for all subconsultant staff. Finance declined an exception, so I escalated to the finance head and the site lead. We agreed to accept it once, with the new format mandatory from the next cycle.

Why it matters: a project manager knows who is responsible at each stage, a Director sees verified packages, and site colleagues know when each document is due.

Lessons I carry

Document everything. A forgotten phone request cost a colleague money. Every request is now written, dated and held in one place.

One accountable contact. A single named person per site is more effective than a whole site sending documents.

Exceptions are one-time and agreed. I accept an exception only with the right people’s agreement, and only once.

Escalate early. Raising a stalled item while there is still time to resolve it helped improve turnaround.
